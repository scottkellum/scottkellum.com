# Creating Shaker Style Websites

_This article contains references to religion, specifically Shakerism. This article is an academic exploration of concepts in these religions and their impact on artifacts. It is not intended to proselytize._

Shakers are known for their simple yet meticulously crafted wares. From furniture to cabinetry, the term “shaker style” has permeated the vernacular of these trades. In the same way that a small apartment is “cozy”, a simple cabinet or chair is “shaker”. But just as “cozy” means so much more than small; it means comfortable, content, restful, and calm. “Shaker” means so much more than simple. At a time with a web ecosystem dominated by large frameworks and large libraries of dependencies that are starting to create [measurable problems for the web](https://infrequently.org/2024/08/the-landscape/), I have been drawn to the question of what a shaker style website might be like?

To start to understand Shaker style, it’s important to understand what Shakers believe. Shakers believe we are stewards of heaven here on earth. They create objects for heaven and the labor that goes into these objects is worship. They prioritize quality, utility, sustainability, and longevity while avoiding superfluous adornment and complexity. The resulting objects are simple, useful, long lasting, and repairable. They do not avoid invention and innovation, but they take care that any innovation does not come at the expense of their values.

### Longevity

There is a timeless beauty to [software that is finished](https://josem.co/the-beauty-of-finished-software/). Software that exists and we do not need to adapt and re-adapt to constantly changing features. [Craigslist](https://craigslist.org/) and [Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/) are great examples of major websites that rarely change while still providing a consistent quality of experience to people over decades. The personal blogs of  [Liz Danzico](https://bobulate.com/) and [John Gruber](https://daringfireball.net/) also provide timeless and unchanging expereances to readers. Expereances that are not distracted by new features and instead focus on content. It is easy to look back at examples of websites with longevity because they have widthstood the test of time. To design for longevity is to design for the distant future, not the distant past.  These websites were all new once and it’s worthwhile to consider the care, craft, and dependencies you invest in them to survive decades into the future.

This isn’t an argument against redesigns. [Lin Fisher](https://lynnandtonic.com/) does a beautiful redesign of her website every year. But redesigning should be a choice, not a need.

### Sustainability

[Energy consumption](https://css-tricks.com/thinking-about-power-usage-and-websites/) is increasingly a concern across tech with a sharp [increase in power demand](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/us-electric-utilities-brace-surge-power-demand-data-centers-2024-04-10/) that is threatening [climate goals](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2024/03/13/climate/electric-power-climate-change.html). Reducing load both on server infrastructure as well as the client would be an important consideration for Shaker style websites. [Low Tech Magazine](https://solar.lowtechmagazine.com/) is a beautifully designed example of a sustainable website that runs on solar power. It is concervitive with both bandwidth and electrical use.
